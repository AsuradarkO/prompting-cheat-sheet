⚡ Token-Efficient Prompting

Cheat sheet — every token should earn its place.

1Lead with the verb

Start with what you want, not background.

"Hi, I was wondering if you could please help me summarize…"
"Summarize in 3 bullets:"

2Dictate the output shape

Constrain length and format up front — it prevents rambling you'll pay to trim later.

"Reply in 5 bullets, max 15 words each."

3Cut politeness padding

"Please," "thank you so much," "I'd really appreciate it" — tokens spent, zero effect on quality.

4Show, don't describe

One 3-line example of the format beats 100 words explaining it.

5Never restate

In an ongoing chat, "same format as above" replaces a whole paragraph.

6Split big tasks

One mega-prompt with 8 requirements → bloated answer. Chain it: draft → "tighten" → "check X."

7Describe fully: goal, audience, format, constraints

The model can't see what you don't say. Every missing detail (who it's for, how long, what tone, what to avoid) gets filled with a guess — and you pay to fix the guess.

"Write an email about the meeting."
"Write a 4-sentence email to my boss: reschedule Tuesday's meeting to Thursday 2pm, polite but brief."

8Discern before you ship

Delegation decides before (what to hand off); Discernment judges after (is this output actually right?). AI is fluent, not honest — it will state guesses with total confidence.

"Verify every fact and number before this goes out — the signature on it is yours, not the model's."

9One persona per prompt

"Strict ATS" and "senior recruiter" in one prompt pull opposite directions — a keyword parser and a human judge score differently. Muddled role, muddled output. Sequence them or pick one.

"Act as an ATS and a senior recruiter…"
"Act as a senior recruiter (not a keyword counter)…"

10No fake-precision scores

A 0–100% "match score" is invented authority — a confident guess dressed as measurement. Ask for the decision the score was supposed to inform instead.

"Give me a Match Score (0-100%)."
"No score — list the 5 gaps that would actually cost me the interview."

11Demand replacement text, not advice

"Suggest how to fix it" leaves the writing on you. Make the model do the work: quote the offending line and hand back the rewritten fix.

"Suggest how to fix each red flag."
"Quote the line, rate severity, give the rewritten replacement text."

12Prohibitions need an outlet

"Don't invent metrics" fighting "be measurable" — under pressure the model quietly invents anyway. Give it the approved alternative: a placeholder you fill in.

"Where a metric would help, write [ADD NUMBER: what to measure]."

13One job per prompt — but different readers count as different jobs

Two prompts with the same inputs aren't duplicates if they model different judges. An ATS parser and a senior recruiter reading the same resume-vs-JD are two stages of the real pipeline, not one question asked twice. Correction 2026-10-12: Vince caught me merging these — the machine gate and the human second look stay split.

14Structure beats adjectives

A sentence-by-sentence spec ("Sentence 2: biggest achievement with data") outperforms adjective stacking ("high-impact, measurable, results-focused"). Tell it the shape, not the vibe.

15Cut the theater, keep the forcing function

The model can't "look for 10 seconds" — it's stage direction. The "single biggest doubt" constraint is what actually does the work. Keep the constraint, drop the play-acting.

16Ask for two variants

Choosing between options beats editing one draft. Two versions cost little and give you something to react against.

"Write two versions — I'll pick."

Demo

Bloated: "Hello! I hope you're well. Could you please write me a short story about a robot? Maybe ~300 words, kind of sad but hopeful? Thank you so much!"

Tight: "Write a 300-word story: a robot, sad but hopeful tone."

Same result, a third of the tokens.
